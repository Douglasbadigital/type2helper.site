Understanding Diabetes: What Every American Should Know

A clear, practical guide to warning signs, risk factors, the numbers that matter, and the everyday habits that help keep blood sugar in a healthy range.

Why This Matters

Diabetes is one of the most common chronic conditions in the United States — and one of the most underdiagnosed. Many people live for years with high blood sugar without noticing any symptoms.

More than 100 million American adults are living with diabetes or prediabetes — and most people with prediabetes don't know they have it.

What Is Diabetes?

Diabetes happens when your body can't use sugar (glucose) from food properly. Normally, a hormone called insulin helps move glucose from your blood into your cells for energy. When this process doesn't work well, sugar builds up in the bloodstream.

Type 1

The body produces little or no insulin. Usually diagnosed in children and young adults.

Type 2

The most common form (about 9 in 10 cases). The body doesn't use insulin well. Risk increases with age.

Prediabetes

Blood sugar is higher than normal, but not yet in the diabetes range. It can often be reversed with lifestyle changes.

Common Warning Signs

Talk to your doctor if you notice any of these:

  • Frequent urination, especially at night
  • Constant thirst or dry mouth
  • Feeling very tired or low on energy
  • Blurry vision
  • Cuts or sores that heal slowly
  • Tingling or numbness in the hands or feet
  • Unexplained weight loss or increased hunger

Are You at Higher Risk?

Your risk of type 2 diabetes is higher if you:

  • Are 45 years of age or older
  • Are overweight, especially around the waist
  • Have a parent, brother, or sister with type 2 diabetes
  • Are physically active less than 3 times a week
  • Have high blood pressure or high cholesterol
  • Ever had diabetes during pregnancy (gestational diabetes)

Know Your Numbers

A simple blood test can tell you where you stand. These are the standard ranges used by U.S. health guidelines:

The A1C test shows your average blood sugar over the past 2–3 months. A diagnosis must always be confirmed by a healthcare professional.

Daily Habits That Help

🥗

Build a Balanced Plate

Fill half your plate with non-starchy vegetables, one quarter with lean protein, and one quarter with whole grains or other complex carbs.

🚶

Move Every Day

Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week — like a brisk 30-minute walk, 5 days a week.

⚖️

Manage Your Weight

Losing just 5% to 7% of your body weight can significantly lower your risk of developing type 2 diabetes.

🥤

Cut Sugary Drinks

Swap soda, sweet tea, and juice for water, sparkling water, or unsweetened beverages.

😴

Sleep Well

Poor sleep can affect how your body handles blood sugar. Aim for 7 or more hours per night.

🩺

Get Checked Regularly

Ask your doctor about screening, especially if you're over 35 or have any of the risk factors above.

When to See a Doctor Right Away

  • Extreme thirst with very frequent urination
  • Nausea, vomiting, or stomach pain along with high blood sugar
  • Confusion, dizziness, or fainting
  • A foot wound that isn't healing or shows signs of infection

If you take diabetes medication, never stop or change it without talking to your doctor first.
